Gulab Jamun

40 min · 6 people · Desserts · India

Gulab jamun is the sweet that ends most celebrations, soft, warm, and soaked in rose scented syrup until it is heavy with it. The secret is not a long list of ingredients. It is khoya that is fresh and soft, a dough handled gently, frying on low heat so the jamuns cook through without darkening too fast, and syrup that is warm, not boiling, when they go in. Rush any part and the jamuns turn hard, cracked, or dry inside. Take your time, and they swell, soften, and shine. Serve them warm, with the syrup spooned over, and watch them disappear.

Ingredients

  • Khoya 250g
  • flour 3 tbsp
  • Cardamom powder 1/2 tsp
  • Sugar 2 cups
  • water 2 cups
  • Rose water 1 tsp
  • 2 drops of lemon
  • Oil / ghee for frying

Method

  1. Crumble the khoya and knead it with the flour and cardamom until a soft, smooth dough forms. Do not over knead, or the jamuns turn dense.
  2. Cover the dough and rest for 15 minutes. It should be soft enough to roll without cracking.
  3. Make the syrup by heating sugar, water and lemon juice until the sugar dissolves. Simmer for 5 minutes, add cardamom and rose water, and keep it warm, not boiling.
  4. Grease your palms lightly with ghee. Roll small, smooth balls, about the size of a walnut, with no cracks on the surface.
  5. Heat oil or ghee on low to medium low heat. The oil should be warm enough to sizzle gently, not hot enough to brown at once.
  6. Add the jamuns in batches, stirring gently with the spoon so they roll and brown evenly, for 6 to 8 minutes, until lightly golden.
  7. Lift the jamuns out and put them straight into the warm syrup. They will sizzle softly and begin to swell.
  8. Rest for at least 1 hour, turning once, so the jamuns drink the syrup evenly. Serve warm, with syrup spooned over.

Chef's tip

The jamuns should have no cracks. Grease your hands lightly with ghee while shaping the balls. The syrup should not be too hot, or the jamuns will harden instead of soaking, and it should not be cold, or they will not absorb it.

Serving

Serves 6, with 3 to 4 jamuns per person in small bowls, with plenty of syrup. Serve warm, garnished with chopped pistachios if you like, and keep extra syrup in a jug for second helpings. Gulab jamun suits Eid, weddings and dinner parties, and it is best eaten with a spoon, straight from the bowl. A cup of chai after it is the classic finish.

Storage and reheating

Keep in the syrup in a covered container in the fridge for up to 4 days. Reheat gently in the syrup on low heat, or in the microwave for 20 to 30 seconds, until warm, not hot. Do not boil them in the syrup, because they break apart. Freeze without syrup for up to 1 month, thaw in the fridge, and warm in fresh syrup before serving.

Ingredient notes

  • Fresh, soft khoya makes soft jamuns. Dry, crumbly khoya needs a spoon of milk to bring the dough together.
  • Flour is a binder only. Too much flour makes hard jamuns, so measure it and do not add extra to fix a sticky dough, rest it instead.
  • Lemon juice in the syrup stops it crystallising as it cools, so the syrup stays clear and smooth.
  • Rose water goes in off the heat, so its scent stays bright instead of boiling away.

Variations

  • Kesar jamun: soak a pinch of saffron in warm milk, add it to the syrup, and garnish with pistachios.
  • Stuffed jamun: press a small piece of dried fruit and nut mix into the centre of each ball before frying.
  • Jamun with ice cream: serve warm jamuns over vanilla ice cream, with warm syrup spooned on top, for a hot and cold dessert.

Common mistakes

  • Frying on high heat. The outside darkens fast and the inside stays raw and dense.
  • Shaping balls with cracks. Syrup gets in unevenly and the jamuns split or turn rough.
  • Putting jamuns into boiling syrup. The surface seals and they stay hard inside.
  • Over kneading the dough. It turns tough and the jamuns lose their softness.

Frequently asked questions

Why are my gulab jamuns hard?

The dough was over kneaded, too much flour was used, or the syrup was too hot. Knead gently, measure the flour, and keep the syrup warm, not boiling.

Can I use milk powder instead of khoya?

Yes. Mix 1 cup of milk powder with 2 tbsp of ghee and 3 to 4 tbsp of warm milk into a soft dough, then follow the same steps. The texture is close, though khoya is softer.

Why did my jamuns break in the syrup?

They were fried too little, the dough was too soft, or the syrup was boiling. Fry until lightly golden and firm, and keep the syrup warm.

Can I make the syrup less sweet?

You can reduce the sugar to 1½ cups, but the syrup must still cover the jamuns. Very thin syrup does not soak in the same way, so do not reduce it much further.
